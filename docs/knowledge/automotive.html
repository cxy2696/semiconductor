Automotive semiconductors: qualification is the moat

A car is now a collection of computers on wheels. Automotive chips grow with electrification and ADAS, but the real barrier is qualification, not performance.

Semiconductor content per vehicle has risen from roughly USD 300–400 a decade ago to USD 600–1,000 in a modern car, and higher for premium electric models. The growth comes from three places: electrification, driver assistance, and the digital cockpit.

Where the chips go

  • Powertrain: SiC or IGBT traction inverters, gate drivers, MCUs. An 800V architecture raises the value of SiC content.
  • ADAS: camera image sensors, radar, LiDAR, and a central SoC or domain controller.
  • Body and comfort: dozens of MCUs, motor drivers, LED drivers and connectivity chips.
  • Infotainment: application processors, display drivers, audio and networking.

Qualification is slow and expensive

Automotive parts must pass AEC-Q100 (or Q101 for discrete devices), support ISO 26262 functional safety up to ASIL-D, and be produced under IATF 16949. The customer then runs its own qualification, often for two to four years before a design is locked in. Once designed in, a part typically stays for five to ten years.

This is why automotive is attractive: design wins create long, sticky revenue. It is also why it is slow: a supplier cannot win a socket with a better datasheet alone. It needs a track record, a quality system and often a local fab.

The supply chain is indirect

Carmakers rarely buy semiconductors directly. Tier-1 suppliers such as Bosch, Continental and Denso integrate them into modules, and the semiconductor vendor may be two steps removed. During the 2021 shortage, this opacity made it hard to match capacity to demand, and it is why OEMs now negotiate directly with chipmakers for critical parts.

What drives the cycle

Automotive semiconductor demand follows vehicle production, but with amplification: inventory builds and corrections are sharp. Electrification adds structural growth, while subsidies and emissions rules can pull demand forward and then leave a hangover.

What to watch

  • Content per vehicle and the SiC share of the inverter.
  • Design-win announcements and lifetime revenue figures.
  • Lead times and distributor inventory.
  • Qualification milestones for new entrants.
  • China localisation: domestic MCUs and SiC are moving fast in the local market.
